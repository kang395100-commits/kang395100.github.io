Technology and test data

Phase separation, corrosion, loss of power. We designed our fuel to solve these problems of existing alcohol blends, and checked the results in tests.

Known problems with alcohol blended gasoline overseas

These are common problems reported with low-alcohol blends such as E10, E15 and E20 in the US, China, India, Brazil and other countries. They are not faults of one product. They are issues any alcohol blend must be designed to handle.

ProblemMain causeWhat can happen in use
Phase separationWater gets in, and alcohol and gasoline do not mix well enoughThe fuel splits into layers, so the engine gets an uneven fuel
Metal corrosionAlcohol that has taken in water, water itself, and oxidation by-productsTanks, pipes, pumps and injectors may corrode
Hard cold startsAlcohol needs more heat to evaporate; vapor pressure not matched to the seasonCars start less easily in winter
Lower fuel economyLess energy per liter than gasolineMore fuel is needed for the same distance
Rubber and seal wearAlcohol acts as a solvent and can make some materials swellHoses and seals change and may leak
Limits in supply and transportAlcohol takes in water, and existing pipelines were not built for itSeparate transport and storage are needed

The U.S. Department of Energy says E10 can be used in all gasoline cars, and E15 in light-duty cars from model year 2001 on. Source: U.S. DOE, Ethanol Blends

The three-layer design of ESOLEAN

Built around 50% alcohol, with three layers that support each other.

  1. Base blendThe alcohol and hydrocarbon parts are made to mix evenly, so the fuel stays uniform even with a high alcohol share.
  2. Boiling point and vapor pressureLight and heavy parts are balanced to match vapor pressure to the season, for easy cold starts and no vapor lock in hot weather.
  3. Functional additive systemRaises the water level the fuel can take, and prevents corrosion, oxidation, poor burning and metal-ion damage.

This is what sets it apart from simply adding alcohol to ready-made gasoline.
